    How it actually works

    Anatomy of one drink

    Caffeine doesn't hit instantly and it doesn't switch off. It's absorbed from your gut over roughly half an hour (peaking about 45 minutes in) and from there your liver clears it at a steady proportional rate.

    That's what a half-life means: every 5 hours, whatever is left gets cut in half. Not a fixed number of mg. A fixed fraction. So the tail is long. An afternoon drink is still a quarter of itself at bedtime, which is exactly the part people miss.

    What it's doing in there: caffeine doesn't add energy, it blocks adenosine, the molecule that accumulates while you're awake and makes you feel sleepy. The tiredness keeps building underneath; you just stop receiving the message. When the caffeine clears, it's all still waiting.

    How much can you have on board and still sleep?

    50 mg

    Falling asleep isn't the same as sleeping well. In a controlled trial, 400 mg of caffeine (a large coffee) measurably disrupted sleep even when it was taken six hours before bed, costing about an hour of sleep the drinkers didn't notice losing. Caffeine suppresses deep, slow-wave sleep first, and that's the part you can't feel going missing.

    So the threshold isn't "the level where I can't drop off". Most people can fall asleep on far more than that. It's the level where caffeine stops eating your sleep quality. Under 50 mg at lights-out is a sane default: about a strong cup of tea still circulating. Sensitive, or waking at 3 a.m. a lot? Try 25. Genuinely sleep like a rock? 100 is defensible.

    Your numbers

    These frame the day and set how fast you burn caffeine off.

    5 h
    Typical adult ≈ 5 h. Smokers clear faster (≈3.5 h); hormonal birth control or pregnancy can push it past 10 h. If you sleep worse than this predicts, raise it.
    50 mg
    How much you can have on board and still fall asleep fine. Lower = stricter.
    400 mg
    400 mg/day is the usual healthy-adult guideline.
